# Chapter 84: Promotion & Career Navigation — Getting to L6 and Beyond

> The engineers who get promoted to Staff aren't always the best engineers.
> They're the ones who understood what "Staff impact" means at their company,
> gathered the right evidence, and presented it in the right language at the
> right time. This chapter is the playbook.

## Why This Chapter Matters

This guide prepares you to pass the L6 interview. But if you're currently an L5
at a company trying to get promoted to L6 (instead of interviewing externally),
the path is different. You need: a promo packet, the right sponsorship, the right
timing, and evidence of impact at the right scope. None of this is taught explicitly.

## Planned Content

### Part 1: What "Staff Level Impact" Actually Means
- L4: executes tasks given to them; impact = output of their code
- L5: defines and executes projects; impact = project outcomes
- L6: changes what the team or org works on; impact = direction, not just execution
- The fundamental shift: from "I built X" to "I changed what we built and why"
- Examples of L6 vs L5 impact framing:
  - L5: "I built the new caching layer, which reduced p99 latency by 40%"
  - L6: "I identified that our caching strategy was wrong for our access pattern,
    proposed and got buy-in for a redesign, led the implementation across 3 teams,
    and reduced p99 latency by 40% — eliminating our biggest customer complaint"

### Part 2: The Promo Packet — Structure and Content
- What it is: a written document submitted to calibration committee
- Typical structure: impact summary + project evidence + peer feedback + manager endorsement
- Impact summary: 3–5 bullet points, each showing L6-level scope and outcome
  - Bad: "Led migration of service X to new infrastructure"
  - Good: "Led org-wide migration of 12 services to new infrastructure, reducing
    infra cost by $2M/year and eliminating a class of production incidents —
    required aligning 4 engineering teams and rewriting the deployment tooling"
- Evidence: metrics, PRs, design docs, incident postmortems, cross-team feedback
- Common mistake: writing about what you did instead of the impact it had

### Part 3: Sponsorship — The Hidden Requirement
- Promotions are rarely self-nominated; they require a sponsor (usually your manager + skip)
- Sponsor's job: advocate for you in calibration, pre-sell your promotion to peers
- How to earn sponsorship: visibility (your work is known beyond your immediate team),
  reliability (you do what you say), and scope creep upward (voluntarily take on more)
- Having the promotion conversation early: "I want to be at L6 in 18 months.
  What does that look like? What am I missing?"
- The biggest mistake: assuming good work automatically leads to promotion without advocacy

### Part 4: The Calibration Process — How Decisions Are Made
- Calibration: your manager presents your case to a committee of peer managers
- Your manager is your representative; they need to know your story cold
- Calibration criteria: impact, scope, influence, technical depth (varies by company)
- "Strong Yes", "Yes", "No", "Strong No" — understanding the bar for each
- Timing: most companies have 2 promo cycles/year; missing a cycle = 6 more months
- Pre-calibration: many promotions are pre-decided before calibration through back-channel
  conversations between managers ("soft yes" before the formal process)

### Part 5: Building the Evidence — What to Do 12 Months Out
- Keep a "brag doc": running log of accomplishments, metrics, cross-team feedback
- Make your work visible: design docs, tech talks, incident postmortems with your name
- Seek stretch assignments that have L6-level scope (cross-team, org-wide impact)
- Get cross-team feedback proactively: peers from other teams carry more weight in calibration
- Close the gaps: if you're strong technically but weak on "influence", volunteer to lead
  a cross-team project; if weak on "communication", write more design docs

### Part 6: When to Interview Externally
- External leveling often moves you up one level (L5 → L6 is a common external jump)
- External interviews reset your perception — you're evaluated fresh, not against your history
- The trade-off: external L6 title but unfamiliar codebase + rebuilding trust vs.
  internal promotion with existing relationships + faster impact
- Using an external offer as a promotion lever: risky but sometimes effective
  ("I have an L6 offer externally; what do you think our timeline looks like here?")
- When to leave: if you've been L5 > 2 years with consistent strong performance and
  no promotion — the environment may not be creating L6 opportunities for you

### Part 7: Google vs. Meta vs. Amazon — How Levels Differ
- Google L6 ≈ Meta E6 ≈ Amazon L7 (roughly)
- Google: emphasis on technical depth + cross-org influence + "Googleyness"
- Meta: emphasis on impact metrics, shipping speed, product sense even for eng roles
- Amazon: emphasis on leadership principles, customer obsession, frugality
- The promo bar shifts with economic conditions: during hiring freezes, bar rises;
  during growth, more L6 slots open up

### Part 8: After L6 — L7 and Principal Engineer
- L7/Principal: impact at the company level, not just org level
- The bar is rarer and more political: fewer than 1% of engineers reach this level
- Characteristics: drives multi-year technical strategy, influences recruiting and culture,
  recognized externally (conference talks, papers, open-source contributions)
- When to pursue vs. when to stay at L6: lifestyle trade-off (more responsibility, more
  visibility, more political navigation)

## The One-Sentence Summary

> "Promotion to L6 = impact at L6 scope (changing direction, not just executing) + a promo packet that shows outcomes not activities + a sponsor who advocates for you in calibration + timing aligned to the promotion cycle — the most common failure mode is doing L6 work without making it visible or framing it at the right level of impact."
